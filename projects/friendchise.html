FriendChise

A workplace operations platform built from real problems at Walker's Doughnuts, used every day to run one production location.

Project Summary

FriendChise is a multi-tenant workplace operations platform: scheduling, recurring tasks, procedures, a QR-based customer menu, and the role permissions needed to run all of that safely across a real business.

It wasn't built as a portfolio piece. It was built to run one specific store, and it's still running that store today. The rest of this page is a straightforward account of why I built it, the decisions behind it, and what running it in production has actually looked like.

The Problem

At Walker's Doughnuts, the things that made a shift run well were rarely written down anywhere. A manager knew how to handle a rush. A senior staff member knew the fastest way to prep for the morning. None of it was documented, so none of it survived when that person wasn't rostered on.

Training was inconsistent because it depended entirely on who happened to be working that day. Procedures lived on paper, in someone's head, or nowhere at all. When a store lost an experienced staff member, it usually lost the knowledge they carried with them too.

"The success of one location should strengthen every location." That's the idea FriendChise is built around — operational knowledge should be captured once and reused, not rediscovered by every new hire and every new shift.

Why I Built FriendChise

I saw this problem firsthand. Our store went from one of the better-performing locations in the franchise to sitting near the bottom of the leaderboard over time, and a lot of that decline traced back to staff turnover, inconsistent training, and knowledge that never made it past the people who had it.

I didn't set out to build a SaaS product. I set out to fix a problem at one store. FriendChise exists because writing procedures down once and making them accessible to every shift was clearly better than relying on tribal knowledge and hoping the right person was working that day.

My Role

I built and maintain FriendChise on my own. There's no team handing me a spec — I'm responsible for figuring out what the store actually needs, designing it, building it, shipping it, and keeping it running.

That's covered product discovery and requirements gathering with the actual staff who use the system, UI/UX design in Figma, database design, full-stack development, API development, authentication, multi-tenant architecture, role-based permissions, deployment, documentation, and automated testing.

The project has also picked up more than 20 open-source contributors over time. I review their pull requests and keep the codebase consistent, which means I'm not just writing code — I'm maintaining standards for other people's code too.

Key Engineering Decisions

Multi-tenant from day one

Even with one active location, I built the data model to isolate each organisation's data from the start. Retrofitting tenant boundaries into a system that wasn't designed for them is far more painful than designing for it early, and FriendChise was always meant to support more than one store.

Role-based access control

Owners, managers, and staff all need different things from the same system. Rather than one flat permission level, I built RBAC so each role only sees the tools relevant to their job, which keeps the interface simpler for staff and safer for the business.

Modular product architecture

Timetable, task management, procedures, and the QR menu are built as separate systems that share a common data layer rather than one tangled feature set. That means I can change how the roster works without risking the menu breaking, which matters a lot once real people depend on the app not breaking.

Automated testing on the risky paths

I didn't write tests everywhere equally. I prioritised coverage for permissions, tenant data boundaries, and validation logic — the places where a bug wouldn't just look bad, it would expose one business's data to another or let someone do something they shouldn't.

Treating it like production software

Because a real store depends on this running, deployment, monitoring, and documentation weren't optional extras. I set up CI, error monitoring, and rate limiting because "it works on my machine" isn't good enough when a manager is trying to build a roster at 7am.

Designing for shift work, not a desk

Staff use this on their phones, mid-shift, often with flour on their hands. Every workflow had to be quick to open, quick to read, and forgiving of interruptions, which shaped a lot of the UI decisions more than any technical constraint did.

Major Product Systems

Interactive timetable

Weekly and daily scheduling so staff know what they're doing without asking a manager. Reduces the constant "what am I doing today" interruptions that used to eat into a manager's time.

Task management

Recurring operational tasks get assigned, tracked, and checked off instead of relying on someone remembering to do them. This is one of the two most-used parts of the system.

Procedures and knowledge base

The core of the platform. Procedures that used to exist only in someone's head or on a scrap of paper are written once and are searchable by anyone on shift.

QR menu generation

A paperless, customer-facing menu accessed by QR code. Simple to update from one place instead of reprinting physical menus every time something changes.

Roster builder

Lets a manager build and adjust staff schedules without falling back to a spreadsheet or a group chat full of shift-swap requests.

Guided onboarding / demo system

New users get walked through the product instead of being handed a login and left to figure it out — important when your users are busy hospitality staff, not software people.

Admin dashboard

Where an owner manages permissions, staff, and settings without needing me to make a database change every time something needs adjusting.

Operational calculators

Small business tools that remove repetitive manual math from day-to-day operations — the kind of thing that seems minor until you're the one doing it by hand every day.

Technical Challenges

Keeping tenant boundaries airtight. Multi-tenant bugs don't fail loudly — they fail by quietly leaking one organisation's data into another's view. I had to be deliberate about scoping every query by organisation rather than relying on the UI to hide things correctly.

Making recurring schedules readable. Once you have multiple people, multiple frequencies, and multiple roles overlapping on a single timetable, the data gets complicated fast. The hard part wasn't storing that data — it was presenting it so a tired staff member could glance at their phone and understand it in a few seconds.

Turning tribal knowledge into a system. The procedures feature sounds simple — store some text — but the real work was structuring it so staff would actually search it and trust it, instead of defaulting back to asking a coworker.

Maintaining a live product while the codebase keeps growing. Once real people are relying on the app every day, every change carries more weight. Reviewing contributor pull requests while keeping the architecture consistent, and not breaking things for the one store that depends on it, has been an ongoing balancing act.

Lessons Learned

Building software for people who aren't software people changed how I think about product decisions. A technically clean solution is worthless if the person on shift doesn't understand it or doesn't have time to use it.

Shipping to a real business also changed my priorities around reliability. It's one thing to have a bug in a side project; it's another when a manager is depending on the roster being correct at 6am. That pressure pushed me to take testing, monitoring, and deployment far more seriously than I would have otherwise.

The biggest lesson is a simple one: knowledge is more valuable when it outlives the person who had it. That idea shaped almost every product decision in FriendChise, and it's the same principle I try to apply to how I write code and documentation now — assume someone else will need to understand this without me in the room.
