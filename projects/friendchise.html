Overview

At Walker's Doughnuts, the things that made a shift run well were rarely written down. A manager knew how to handle a rush; a senior staff member knew the fastest way to prep for the morning, and none of it survived when that person wasn't rostered on. Our store slid from one of the better performers in the franchise toward the bottom of the leaderboard, and a lot of that traced back to turnover and knowledge that never outlived the person who had it.

ProblemOperational knowledge, standards, training, and workflows fragment across locations and staff, and disappear when someone leaves.
SolutionA shared operations platform for tasks, scheduling, procedures, tools, and staff coordination, captured once, reused by every shift.

"The success of one location should strengthen every location." I didn't set out to build a SaaS product. I set out to fix this at one store, and FriendChise is still running that store today.

Product

Timetable, tasks, procedures, and tools are built as separate systems sharing one org-scoped data layer, so screenshots below reflect what a real shift actually sees.

Run the week

Weekly and daily scheduling so staff know what they're doing without asking a manager. Overlapping shifts and roles are grouped into readable cards instead of a wall of stacked entries, which removes the constant "what am I doing today" interruptions that used to eat into a manager's time.

Standardise operational knowledge

Recurring tasks, prep procedures, and recipes that used to exist only on paper or in someone's head are written once and searchable by anyone on shift. Tags, comments, and recurrence rules turn a scrap of tribal knowledge into something the whole team can trust.

Train and support staff

Conversion tools, operational calculators, and item lists remove repetitive manual math from day-to-day work, the kind of thing that seems minor until you're the one doing it by hand every shift, and a big part of onboarding someone new quickly.

Coordinate across locations

Each location runs as its own organisation with its own staff, schedule, and data, while a parent organisation can connect multiple franchisees and share brand-level resources, the structure the platform needs before it can grow past one store.

Engineering Challenges

Franchise hierarchy vs. tenant isolation

Problem

The platform needed to support both an independent single store and a franchise network, without one org ever seeing another's data by default.

Decision

A self-referencing parentId relation on Organization lets a parent connect multiple franchisees and share brand-level resources, while every query is still scoped by orgId rather than relying on the UI to hide things correctly.

Result

One data model supports both a single independent location and a multi-location franchise, with tenant boundaries that don't depend on the interface behaving correctly.

Role-based access at org scale

Problem

Owners, managers, and staff need different tools from the same system, and permission logic scattered across screens is exactly where multi-tenant bugs hide.

Decision

Permissions are modelled as org-scoped Role → Permission → MemberRole join tables, checked centrally in a shared authz layer rather than per-page.

Result

Each role only sees the tools relevant to its job, and permission checks are covered directly by the largest slice of the test suite (151 server-action tests) instead of being trusted to the UI.

Making overlapping schedules readable

Problem

Once multiple people, frequencies, and roles overlap on one timetable, five stacked task cards in the same slot become unreadable on a phone mid-shift.

Decision

I introduced grouped timetable cards so overlapping entries render as one group that expands into individual items on demand, instead of exposing every entry at once.

Result

A tired staff member can glance at their phone and understand the day in seconds; complexity is handled by the system, not passed to the user.

Shared UI state for a contextual sidebar

Problem

The ActionSidebar renders different content depending on the current action (grouped timetable cards, conversion workflows) and could stay open while the underlying data changed, leaving it tied to a stale snapshot.

Decision

Rather than patching individual state updates, I moved to a controlled re-render function that reopens the sidebar with fresh content whenever its source data changes.

Result

A single reusable pattern for one shared UI surface depending on many parts of the app, instead of one-off fixes per screen.

Multi-Tenant Model

Franchise hierarchyOrganization ↔ parent/child Organizations, so a franchisee stays independent while a parent can connect and manage multiple locations.
Access controlMembership → MemberRole → Role → Permission, all scoped to one Organization, so access never crosses tenant boundaries.
Org-scoped resourcesTasks, timetables, rosters, tools, and menus each carry an orgId, so every query is scoped at the data layer, not the UI.
55 Prisma modelsCovering auth, organisations, tasks, scheduling, rostering, tools, menus, comments, and admin/audit data.

Testing & Reliability

219

Business logic & validation

Service-layer and Zod validator tests covering scheduling rules, tenant-scoped data validation, and permission logic.

151

Server actions

Permission-gated mutations for tasks, timetables, rosters, and organisation changes, the surface where an authorization bug would matter most.

66

Integration (real Postgres)

Runs against an actual Postgres instance in CI to catch issues unit tests with mocked data can't.

CI pipeline

GitHub Actions runs type-check + lint, unit tests, integration tests against Postgres, and Playwright end-to-end tests as separate gated stages on every pull request.

This isn't full coverage of the codebase; it's concentrated on the paths where a bug wouldn't just look bad, it would leak one organisation's data into another's view or let someone do something their role shouldn't allow.

My Role

I built and maintain FriendChise on my own. There's no team handing me a spec, I own the product direction, the architecture, and every decision described on this page:

  • Product discovery and requirements gathering directly with the staff who use the system
  • UI/UX design in Figma, full-stack development, and API design
  • Database modelling, the 55-model Prisma schema and its migrations
  • Multi-tenant architecture, RBAC, and permission design
  • Test strategy across unit, integration, and end-to-end suites
  • Deployment, CI/CD, error monitoring, and rate limiting

The project has also picked up open-source contributors over time. I review every pull request and own the architecture and product decisions myself, contributors extend the codebase, they don't set its direction, and every contribution still goes through me before it ships.
